**Physics intro**
Nothing pushes the marble. Everything it does on the track comes from the energy it has at the start, and that energy keeps changing form all the way to the finish.

**Gravitational potential energy**
Stored energy an object has because of its height.
Ep = m × g × h, where m = mass (kg), g = 10 m/s², h = height (m)
On The Long Drop: the marble has the most GPE at the top of the runway. That is the only energy it ever gets, so the higher it starts, the more energy it has to spend on the rest of the track.

**Kinetic energy**
Energy an object has because it is moving.
Ek = ½ × m × v², where m = mass (kg), v = speed (m/s)
On The Long Drop: rolling down a drop, GPE turns into KE and the marble speeds up, so it is fastest at the bottom of each drop. Climbing the loop or the kick, KE turns back into GPE and the marble slows down.

**Friction**
A force that acts against motion when surfaces rub or roll against each other. Air resistance is friction with the air.
Energy lost = energy at start − energy left. The lost energy becomes heat and sound.
On The Long Drop: the card, the tape joins and the air all slow the marble, turning some of its kinetic energy into heat and sound. The rolling noise in our video is energy leaving the marble. This is why it eventually stops at the finish.

**Energy at each point**
A — Start. The marble sits still at the top of the tower. All of its energy is GPE.
B — The big drop. Falling down the tower, GPE turns into KE and the marble speeds up fast.
C — Bottom of the drop. The marble reaches the loop with lots of KE. It needs this speed to make it all the way round.
D — Top of the loop. Climbing the loop turns KE back into GPE, so the marble slows right down. It must still be moving to stay on the track.
E — Shallow path. A gentle downhill slope with a sharp corner. The marble speeds up on the straight, loses most of its speed hitting the corner, then speeds up again.
F — Steep drop. The last big drop turns most of the remaining GPE into KE. This is the fastest speed we measured.
G — Kick upwards. The track curves up and launches the marble onto the disc. Going up turns some KE back into GPE, so it slows down.
H — The disc. The marble rolls down the disc into the hole in the middle, spins around inside it, then drops through.
I — Finish. After dropping through the hole, the marble rolls down a short path to the end. Friction turns the last of its KE into heat and sound, and it stops.

**Numbers used in the calculations**
Marble mass — 8 grams = 0.008 kg (estimate)
Gravity — 10 m/s²
Start height — 152 cm = 1.52 m (measured)
Speed near the bottom — about 1 m/s (read off the test video)

**Calculation 1 — GPE at the top**
Gpe = m × g × h
8/1000 × 10 × 1.52 = 0.12 J
This is all the energy the marble ever gets.

**Calculation 2 — fastest it could possibly go**
Ek = ½mv² rearranged to v = √(2Ek ÷ m)
√(2 × 0.12 ÷ 0.008) = 5.5 m/s
If every joule of GPE turned into KE, the marble would reach 5.5 m/s.

**Calculation 3 — why it never reaches 5.5 m/s**
Real speed is about 1 m/s, so real speed < maximum speed.
Friction with the card, the tape joins, the bang into the corner and air resistance turn most of the energy into heat and sound. Some also goes into spinning the marble instead of moving it forward.

**Note on the calculations**
The starting height is measured. The marble's mass is an estimate of 8 grams, and the real speed is read off our test video, so these answers are close estimates rather than exact values.

**Why the loop slows the marble so much**
On a straight section, the track only has to hold up the marble's weight. In the loop, the track also has to push the marble round in a circle, so the marble presses much harder into the card. The harder two surfaces press together, the more friction there is.
Our loop also has tape joins for the marble to bump over, and the thin card flexes as the marble passes, soaking up even more energy.

**Why the marble can't climb higher than where it started**
Energy can't be created or destroyed, only changed from one form to another. This is the law of conservation of energy. The marble's total energy is set at the start, so to reach a higher point it would need more GPE than it ever had.
Friction takes some energy away along the way, so the marble can't even get back up to its starting height. A loop is harder still: the marble must still be moving at the top to stay on the track, so the top of the loop has to sit well below the start.

**Going deeper — height matters, not length**
A runway's length doesn't add any energy. Only the height the marble drops does. Our long runway helps because it starts high, but every extra centimetre of track also adds friction.

**Going deeper — rolling uses energy too**
A rolling marble also spins. Some of its energy goes into spinning instead of moving forward, so even with no friction it travels a little slower than something sliding down the same drop.

**Going deeper — the loop height rule**
With no friction, a rolling marble must start at least 2.7 times the loop's radius above the bottom of the loop to make it round. With friction, it needs to start even higher.

**Evaluation intro**
What worked, what didn't, and what we would change if we built The Long Drop again.

**Strengths**
The high starting runway gave the marble plenty of speed for the loop and the rest of the track.
A 5.3 second run, well over the 3 second minimum, while staying within the card and straw limits.

**Weaknesses**
The loop loses an unusually large amount of speed to friction.
The loop and the section below it took several rebuilds before they held their shape under the weight of the track.

**Next time**
Rebuild the loop from one smooth strip of card, as round as possible, with tape only on the outside so the marble never rolls over a join.
Plan both halves together before building, so the joins line up first time and we spend less time rebuilding.

**Real roller coasters — lift hills**
A chain pulls the train to the top of the first hill, giving it all its GPE. Our starting height does the same job.

**Real roller coasters — teardrop loops**
Real loops are taller than they are wide, with a tighter curve at the top. This keeps the forces on riders lower than a perfect circle would.

**Real roller coasters — brakes**
At the end of the ride, brakes use friction or magnets to turn the train's KE into heat, just like friction stops our marble at the finish.
