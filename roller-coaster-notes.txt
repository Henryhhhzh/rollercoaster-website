Roller coaster notes - Year 9 STEM (Henry, working with Michael)

The task was to design and build a model roller coaster out of card, paper straws and masking tape that you roll marbles down. The rules were at least one loop, the run has to last at least 3 seconds, no more than 8 sheets of A4 card, max 20 straws, the whole thing has to fit on an A3 base and stand up by itself, and it has to work at least 3 times. We also had to give it a name and a logo.

We called ours The Long Drop because the first drop is the whole point of it. The marble starts 152 cm above the base, which is way higher than anything else in the class, and everything the marble does after that comes from that one drop.

Who did what. I built the whole top section above the loop, including the long starting channel, and all the straw supports for the entire coaster. Michael built the whole bottom half, so the loop, the steep drop, the kick and the disc. We built the two halves at the same time and then joined them together at the end with supports, which saved us heaps of time, although the joins didn't line up perfectly first go and we had to redo a couple of them.

How the track actually works, in order. The marble starts at the top of the tower 152 cm up. It drops down a long straight channel, which is the big drop. At the bottom of the tower it goes round the loop. Then it comes out onto a shallow path that runs along the top of the frame, turns a sharp corner and goes down the left side. Then it goes down the big steep slide, and at the bottom of that the track curves back up into a kick that launches it onto the disc. It rolls down the disc into the hole in the middle, spins around inside the hole for about three quarters of a second, drops through, lands on a short path underneath and rolls along that to the end, where it stops.

Materials we used: 8 A4 sheets of card, which is the maximum allowed, 20 straws, also the maximum, one A3 sheet for the base, and masking tape.

Timing. We wrote down 5.3 seconds. Being honest that number is the length of the video clip we filmed rather than a stopwatch time. The marble is already moving when the clip starts and it goes out of sight under the ramp near the end, so the actual run is somewhere around 5 seconds. Either way it is well over the 3 second minimum so it passes.

Testing. We filmed 10 clips of the finished coaster. 6 of them are complete runs where the marble goes the whole way through the hole and along the final path. 2 of them failed - in one the marble came off the track before it even reached the base, and in the other it stopped right at the edge of the disc and never went into the hole. Of the last 2 clips, in one we stopped filming too early while the marble was still going round the disc, and the other one was just us setting the coaster up. So 6 out of 8 proper runs finished, which is more than enough for the "has to work 3 times" rule.

Calculations. Mass of the marble is estimated at 8 grams, so 0.008 kg. Gravity 10 m/s². Height measured at 152 cm which is 1.52 m.

Gpe = mgh = 8/1000 x 10 x 1.52 = 0.12 J.

That 0.12 J is every bit of energy the marble is ever going to have. Nothing pushes it, there's no motor, you just let go of it at the top, so the entire rest of the ride has to be paid for out of that 0.12 J.

Then if you assume all of the GPE turns into kinetic energy by the bottom, you can work out the fastest the marble could possibly be going. Ek = ½mv², rearranged that's v² = 2Ek ÷ m, so v = √(2Ek ÷ m) = √(2 x 0.12 ÷ 0.008) = √30 = 5.5 m/s.

It never actually gets anywhere near 5.5 m/s. Off the video it's doing roughly 1 m/s near the bottom of the steep drop (the video records 30 frames every second, so you can count frames between two points and work out the time). At 1 m/s the kinetic energy is ½ x 0.008 x 1² = 0.004 J. We started with 0.12 J and by the bottom the marble only has about 0.004 J of movement energy left, so something like 95% of the energy has gone somewhere else.

Where it goes is friction, and that's the whole answer to "why doesn't it reach the theoretical speed". The marble rubs against the card the whole way down, it bumps over every piece of tape at every join, it bangs into the side wall at the sharp corner and loses most of its speed right there, the thin card flexes under it and absorbs energy, and there's air resistance on top of that. All of that turns kinetic energy into heat and sound. The rolling noise you can hear on the video is literally energy leaving the marble. There's also a bit that isn't lost but isn't useful either - a rolling marble has to spin as well as move forward, so some of the energy goes into spinning it rather than making it go faster in a straight line.

The three types of energy we had to explain, in terms of our coaster.

Gravitational potential energy is the stored energy something has because of its height, Ep = mgh. Ours is at its maximum at the very top of the tower, 152 cm up, and it's the only energy the marble gets given. The higher we built the start, the more energy there was to spend on the rest of the track, which is exactly why we made the tower as tall as we could get away with.

Kinetic energy is the energy something has because it's moving, Ek = ½mv². As the marble drops, GPE turns into KE and it speeds up, so it's fastest at the bottom of each drop. Going up anything - the loop, the kick - KE turns back into GPE and it slows down. Because the speed is squared in the formula, doubling the speed gives you four times the kinetic energy, which is why the loop needs so much speed going into it.

Friction is a force that acts against the motion whenever two surfaces rub or roll on each other, and air resistance is basically friction with the air. It doesn't destroy the energy, it converts it into heat and sound, which is energy we can't get back and can't use. It's the reason the marble eventually stops at the end instead of rolling forever, and the reason it can never climb back up to the height it started at.

Going through the energy at each point of the track:

- At the top of the tower, the marble is sitting still, so it's 100% GPE and no KE.
- Going down the big drop, GPE turns into KE and it speeds up fast.
- At the bottom of the drop it's got the most KE it's going to have, and it needs all of it to make it round the loop.
- At the top of the loop, KE has turned back into GPE, so this is the slowest point in the loop. It still has to be moving here or it falls off the track.
- On the shallow path, it's a gentle slope so it picks up speed slowly, then loses most of it hitting the corner and has to build it back up again.
- Down the steep drop, most of the remaining GPE becomes KE. This is the fastest we measured it going, about 1 m/s.
- On the kick it goes upwards, so some KE turns back into GPE and it slows down.
- On the disc it rolls down into the hole, spins around in the hole, and drops through.
- On the short path at the end, friction turns the last of the KE into heat and sound and it stops.

Why the loop takes so much speed off it. On a flat piece of track, the track only has to hold the marble's weight up. In the loop, the track also has to push the marble round in a circle, so the marble is pressing much harder into the card than it normally does. The harder two surfaces press together, the more friction there is between them, so the loop is easily the worst part of the track for losing energy. On top of that our loop has tape joins the marble bumps over and the card flexes when the marble goes round it.

Why the marble can't get higher than where it started. Energy can't be created or destroyed, only changed from one form to another, which is the law of conservation of energy. The marble's total energy is set the moment you let go of it at the top. To get to a point higher than that, it would need more GPE than it ever had, which is impossible. And because friction takes some of it along the way, it can't even get back to its original height. A loop is harder again, because the marble has to still be moving at the top of the loop to stay on the track, so the top of the loop has to be well below the starting point.

A couple of other things worth knowing. The length of the runway doesn't add energy on its own, only the height it drops through does - a long flat runway would add friction and give nothing back. And for a loop there's a rule that a rolling marble needs to start at least 2.7 times the loop's radius above the bottom of the loop to make it round without friction, and with friction it needs more than that, which is why the loop is near the bottom of our tower and not near the top.

Evaluation. What worked well was the height. The tall start gave us so much energy to play with that even after losing most of it to friction there was still enough left to get through the loop, the slide, the kick, the disc and the final path. Getting a 5+ second run while staying inside the 8 sheets and 20 straws limit worked out too.

What didn't work as well. The loop scrubs off a huge amount of speed, more than it should, because of the tape joins and how much the marble presses into the card going round it. The sharp corner on the shallow path is also bad - the marble smacks into the side wall there and comes out much slower than it went in. And the loop and the section under it took a few rebuilds before they were strong enough to hold their shape under the weight of the track.

If we did it again I'd rebuild the loop out of one smooth strip of card, as round as possible, with the tape only on the outside so the marble never has to roll over a join. I'd round off that sharp corner into a curve instead of a right angle. And I'd plan both halves together before starting to build, so the joins line up the first time instead of the third.

Real roller coasters use the exact same physics. The chain that pulls the train up the first hill is doing the same job as our starting height - it's giving the train all the GPE it's going to have for the whole ride. Real loops are teardrop shaped rather than circular, taller than they are wide with a tighter curve at the top, which keeps the forces on the riders down. And the brakes at the end of a real ride use friction, or magnets, to turn the train's kinetic energy into heat, which is exactly what friction does to our marble at the end of the short path, just on purpose.

Name and logo. The Long Drop, named after the 152 cm first drop, since that's the thing that makes ours different from everyone else's. Logo drawn in VectorPea as a vector so it can be scaled up without going pixelated.
